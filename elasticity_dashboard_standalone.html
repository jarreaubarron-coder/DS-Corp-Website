Cross Price Elasticity (XED) Dashboard

Explore short-run and long-run elasticities with distributed lags (0 to 3 quarters).

Plain-English guide: how to read this dashboard

The question. When the price of something (or a company's revenue) goes up, do job postings go up or down, and by how much? This dashboard measures that link for software jobs, overall jobs, and a merged jobs index, using either NVIDIA's quarterly revenue or the prices businesses pay for data processing services (PPI) as the comparison. The measure is called cross-price elasticity: the percent change in job postings that goes with a 1% change in the other variable.

Reading the numbers

  • Negative: job postings tend to fall when the other variable rises. Positive: they tend to rise with it.
  • Size: a value of −0.2 means a 1% rise goes with about a 0.2% drop in postings. Anything beyond 1 (up or down) is a strong reaction.
  • Timing: Lag 0 is the same quarter; Lags 1 to 3 are one to three quarters later. The Long-Run number adds them all up to give the total effect over a year.

The four boxes and two charts

  • Long-Run and Immediate: the total effect over a year, and the effect right away.
  • R-squared: how much of the ups and downs in job postings the model accounts for. Closer to 1 means more.
  • Durbin-Watson: a reliability check. Below 1.5 means the model's misses are linked from one period to the next, so treat the results as rough.
  • Bar chart: the effect at each timing. Line chart: both series squeezed onto a 0–1 scale so their shapes can be compared.

Example. With the starting selection (Software Job Postings and NVIDIA Quarterly Revenue), a 1% rise in NVIDIA's revenue goes with roughly a 1% drop in software postings in the same quarter. A rebound three quarters later offsets part of that, so the net effect over a year is smaller, about −0.4%.

Be careful. These are patterns, not proof that one thing causes the other: both series also move with the wider economy and with time. Many of the individual lag results are not statistically strong, and every combination here has a Durbin-Watson below 1.5, so the numbers are best used to compare directions and rough sizes rather than as exact predictions.
