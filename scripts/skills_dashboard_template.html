Job Skills Demand Dashboard

Which skills do employers need, and how has demand for them moved since February 2020? Indeed job-posting trends by job family, linked to the skills the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database attaches to each job title.

Plain-English guide: how to read this dashboard

The question. Indeed publishes how many job postings there are over time for job families such as Software Development and Data & Analytics, but it does not say which skills each posting asks for. O*NET, the government's occupational database, does the opposite: it rates how important each of 35 skills is for each job title (for example, how much a Software Developer needs Programming or Mathematics), but it has no dates. This dashboard joins the two. It asks: if postings for the jobs that rely on a skill rise or fall, what happens to the demand for that skill?

How the skill lines are built

  • Each Indeed job family is matched to a set of O*NET job titles (see the job-title table at the bottom).
  • For every skill, O*NET gives an importance score in each family, from 1 (not important) to 5 (extremely important).
  • A skill's line is a blend of three posting trends: Software Development, Data & Analytics, and all job postings. Each trend counts in proportion to how much that skill matters in it.
  • So Programming follows the tech families closely, Mathematics leans toward Data & Analytics, and skills like Service Orientation or Repairing mostly follow the overall job market.
  • The dashed grey line is all job postings, the benchmark. A skill line below it means postings for jobs that rely on that skill have fallen further than the overall market.

What you can do

  • Pick up to six skills with the buttons, or click any row of the scoreboard.
  • Change the date range and choose whether the lines restart at 100 on the first day (easier to compare) or keep Indeed's February 2020 base.
  • Skill profile chart: see which skills a job family needs most, and which stand out compared with the average job.
  • Scoreboard: click a column title to sort all 35 skills by change in demand.

Be careful. The skill lines are an estimate, not a count of postings that mention a skill. They can only differ from each other as much as the three Indeed trends differ (and Data & Analytics and Software Development have moved very similarly), and they assume the skills a job title needs stayed the same over time. Treat them as a way to compare which skills sit with growing or shrinking parts of the job market, not as exact measurements. More detail is in "How this was built" at the bottom.

How this was built, and its limits

Indeed data. Three U.S. daily posting indexes (7-day averages, February 1, 2020 = 100): Data & Analytics, Software Development, and All postings. The files do not list individual job titles, so the link to titles goes through O*NET.

O*NET data. Version , 35 skills (10 essential and 25 transferable) rated for 894 occupations. Importance is rated 1 to 5 by occupational analysts. Each Indeed family is represented by the O*NET occupations listed in the job-title table, chosen by matching the families' typical job titles (such as software engineer and data analyst) to O*NET's title lists. Every occupation counts equally; O*NET has no hiring volumes to weight them.

Skill demand index. For a skill with O*NET importance wDA in Data & Analytics jobs, wSW in Software Development jobs and wALL in the average of all 894 occupations (each rescaled to 0–100), the index on a given day is (wDA × DA index + wSW × SW index + wALL × ALL index) / (wDA + wSW + wALL). The three groups count equally; no posting volumes are available to weight them. Change over a range compares the average of the first 30 days with the average of the last 30 days.

Limits. (1) Only two job families have posting data, so every skill line is a mix of the same three trends, and the two tech families have moved almost identically. (2) O*NET ratings are a snapshot, so the lines show how demand for jobs that use a skill moved, not how the skill requirements of postings changed. (3) O*NET has not yet rated some titles Indeed would file here, such as Data Scientists, so Business Intelligence Analysts, Statisticians and Operations Research Analysts stand in. (4) The all-postings benchmark covers every occupation. Adding more Indeed job families would make the skill lines more distinct.

Sources: Indeed Hiring Lab job postings index (via FRED for Software Development and All postings); O*NET 30.3 Database (U.S. Department of Labor/ETA, CC BY 4.0).
